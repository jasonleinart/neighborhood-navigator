# Neighborhood Navigator: Project Context

## Origin

This project started from the Villages CDC Program Manager job listing (February 2026). Two responsibilities stood out:

> **5.** Work with residents in applying for homeowner and resident resources such as property tax exemptions, lead abatement, utility assistance, home loans, grants so as to prevent population loss. In this activity, track and attempt to quantify what programs need more funding.
>
> **6.** Work with residents in concert with ED on infrastructure advocacy projects.

Point 5 describes two distinct jobs packed into one sentence: (1) help residents navigate programs, and (2) track unmet demand to justify funding. No small CDC has the tools to do both. That's the problem this tool solves.

After an introductory meeting with Mac (VCDC Executive Director), the scope of the tracking problem became clear. This isn't a VCDC problem. It's a citywide problem worth $63M/year with no shared infrastructure.

## The Problem: Detroit's Home Repair Data Gap

### The Numbers

| Metric | Figure | Source |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Total spent on home repair (2024) | $63M | Detroit Home Repair Census |
| Homes served (2024) | 2,628 | Detroit Home Repair Census |
| Organizations involved | 30+ | Detroit Home Repair Census |
| Unique programs | 31 | Detroit Home Repair Census |
| Separate funding sources | 34 | Detroit Home Repair Census |
| Estimated total repair need | $2B-$4B | U-M Poverty Solutions (2021) |
| Families in substandard housing | 38,000 | U-M Poverty Solutions |
| Current spending as % of need | ~3% annually | Derived |

At 2,628 homes/year against 38,000 in substandard conditions, it takes 14.5 years to reach everyone — assuming no new homes deteriorate. They will.

### The ARPA Cliff

43.5% of 2024 home repair funding (~$22M) came from ARPA, which expires in 2026. Post-ARPA annual spend drops to an estimated $30-40M. The $63M year was the high-water mark, not the baseline.

### The Fragmentation

31 programs from 34 funding sources across 30+ organizations, with no shared intake, no common case management, and no outcomes tracking across providers. Before the 2024 Home Repair Census (the first of its kind), there was no citywide data on who was being served, by whom, or with what results. Even in that census, only ~$50M of the $63M could be confirmed with high accuracy — organizations couldn't fully verify their own spending.

BridgeDetroit hosted a Community Conversation (August 2024) where panelists described the system as "extremely hard to navigate." One speaker compared it to "trying to fit a round peg in a square hole."

## The Tax Foreclosure Pipeline

Home repair is upstream of tax foreclosure. When residents can't maintain their homes, property values decline, tax burdens become unsustainable relative to home value, and foreclosure follows.

### Scale of Damage

| Period | Statistic |
|--------|-----------|
| 2002-2019 | 135,000+ Detroit properties foreclosed at least once |
| 2005-2015 | ~140,000 homes foreclosed; ~110,000 were tax (not mortgage) foreclosures |
| 2011-2015 | 1 in 4 Detroit properties went through tax foreclosure |
| Since 2009 | 100,000+ Detroiters displaced by foreclosure |
| 2024 | 2,111 total Wayne County foreclosures (lowest since 2003, down 89% from 2015) |

The decline is real and prevention programs deserve credit. But the damage from peak years is generational.

### The Overassessment Factor

Research by Atuahene & Berry (UC Irvine Law Review) found Detroit overassessed 55-85% of properties between 2009-2015, violating Michigan's constitutional cap. An estimated 10% of all tax foreclosures were caused by illegally inflated assessments. For the cheapest homes (under $9,000), that figure jumps to 25%.

### HOPE: The Participation Gap

The Homeowners Property Exemption reduces current-year property taxes by 10-100% based on household income, saving typical homeowners $1,500-3,000/year.

| HOPE Metric | Data |
|-------------|------|
| Applications (2023) | 21,384 (up 77% from 2020) |
| Approved exemptions | 10,000-15,000/year |
| Estimated eligible (2017) | ~40,000 residents |

The critical finding: Rocket Community Fund's Neighbor to Neighbor project sent 400+ surveyors to visit 65,000 Detroit homes in tax distress. They found **87% of the 14,000 tax-delinquent homeowners they interviewed qualified for HOPE**. These were people already in the foreclosure pipeline who could have been pulled out with an existing program. The bottleneck was awareness and navigation, not funding.

### Cost of Vacancy vs. Prevention

| Cost Category | Amount |
|---------------|--------|
| Average demolition | $21,556 per structure |
| Total demolition bond repayment | $450-490M |
| Illegal dumping cleanup | $5M/year |
| Property value depression from vacancy | Up to 20% for surrounding homes |
| Remaining vacant units | ~81,000 off-market; ~175,000 vacant properties total |

**The math:** For the cost of demolishing one house ($21,556), you could fund 7-14 years of HOPE tax exemption for one household, or one year of exemption for 7-14 households. Michigan's Hardest Hit Fund allocated ~$380M to demolitions — money originally intended for homeowner foreclosure prevention.

### The Feedback Loop

The pipeline is self-reinforcing:

1. Aging housing stock deteriorates (median Detroit home built 1940s-1950s)
2. Owners can't afford repairs on fixed/low income
3. Properties lose value while tax assessments lag
4. Tax delinquency begins (forfeiture after 1 year)
5. Foreclosure (after 2 years under Michigan General Property Tax Act)
6. Auction — between 2011-2015, only 18% of auctioned properties were current on taxes afterward
7. Vacancy, blight, crime increase, neighboring values drop
8. Cycle repeats for adjacent properties

## How Other Cities Handle This

### St. Louis Home Repair Network (most direct analog)

The most direct parallel to Detroit. Multiple housing nonprofits formed a new umbrella organization with a **single centralized intake**. Instead of homeowners applying to six different agencies with separate waitlists, one common application routes them to whoever can best serve them.

How it came together:
- 18-month process through a ULI Partnership for Health Equity program
- UMSL provided the policy research backbone
- UMSL surveyed 583 older homeowners and quantified $13,023 average unmet repair cost per household, $300M+ citywide total
- That number gave CDCs a concrete figure to put in front of funders — before the study, they had anecdotes

The key insight: **the research came first, then the coordination infrastructure.** They didn't build a platform and hope orgs would adopt it. They produced a number that made the fragmentation problem undeniable, and the common intake system followed as the obvious fix.

Still in development. They're designing the single intake form now. Not yet operational. Detroit isn't behind — both cities are at roughly the same stage of recognizing the problem.

### Houston "The Way Home" (homelessness, but the infrastructure model)

The gold standard, but for homelessness rather than home repair. Worth studying because the infrastructure model is what Detroit's home repair ecosystem lacks.

What they built:
- 100+ partner organizations sharing a single HMIS database
- Standardized intake assessments across all providers
- Public dashboards updated monthly showing system-wide outcomes
- Coordinated access — one front door, centralized matching to available resources

Results: 57% cumulative reduction in homelessness since 2011. 25,000+ individuals housed. Nearly 90% still housed two years later.

How it happened:
- Started in 2012 with a full system redesign — restructured governance, built accountability models, analyzed resource inventory
- Launched coordinated access in 2014
- The political will came from Houston's mayor making homelessness reduction a public priority with measurable targets

**The translation problem for Detroit home repair:** HMIS was purpose-built for homelessness workflows (assessment → shelter → housing → follow-up). Home repair has a different workflow (screening → scoping → contractor matching → inspection → completion). Nobody has adapted the HMIS model for home repair. That's the gap in the market.

Houston also had **top-down political commitment** driving adoption. Detroit's home repair ecosystem is bottom-up — 30+ independent orgs with no single authority compelling them to share data. That's a fundamentally harder coordination problem.

### Detroit's Own Efforts (Already Underway)

This is the part that matters most for the D4 conversation — understanding what already exists before proposing something new.

**Citywide Home Repair Task Force + Data Driven Detroit**
- Produced the 2024 Home Repair Census — the first citywide data on who's spending what
- 30+ organizations submitted data through a web portal across three reporting periods
- Retrospective, not real-time. Tells you what happened last year, not what's happening now
- The portal exists and the relationships with the 30+ orgs are established

**Detroit Housing HelpLine** (launched May 2023)
- ~6,700 calls/month as a centralized entry point
- Built with Harvard Kennedy School's Government Performance Lab
- Functions as triage and referral — "you should call Wayne Metro for weatherization"
- Does not track whether the referral resulted in service. No closed loop.

**Detroit Housing Network**
- Referral coordination across providers
- No shared case management or outcomes tracking

**The three pieces that don't connect:** Detroit has a retrospective data collection system (Census), a centralized phone intake that refers but doesn't track outcomes (HelpLine), and a referral network with no shared database (Housing Network). The missing layer is real-time, intake-to-outcome tracking across providers. The Census proves the data is valuable. The HelpLine proves centralized intake works. Neither closes the loop from "resident called" to "resident was served" or "resident is still waiting."

### What Doesn't Exist Yet

No one — in Detroit or any other city — has built a real-time, shared intake-to-outcome tracking system purpose-built for home repair coordination across multiple CDCs. The HMIS model from homelessness is the closest analog but hasn't been adapted for home repair workflows (screening, scoping, contractor matching, inspection, completion).

The St. Louis common application is closest to filling this gap, and it's not built yet either.

### Technology Landscape

| Platform | What It Does | Problem |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| findhelp (fka Aunt Bertha) | Resource directory + referral tracking | Good for discovery. No shared case management. |
| Unite Us | Closed-loop referral network | Healthcare-oriented. Expensive. |
| Bitfocus Clarity Human Services | HMIS + coordinated entry | Closest existing product but wrong workflow (homelessness, not home repair). |
| NYC Benefits Screening API | Open API screening against 40+ programs | Discovery only, no tracking. |

### Strategic Questions for D4

These are the questions worth bringing to Francis and Sam. They've been in this ecosystem long enough to know where the real friction is.

- **Who owns it?** Data Driven Detroit has the org relationships. The HelpLine has the call volume. D4 has the policy leverage. The City has the authority. A new tool from an outsider doesn't automatically fit anywhere in this landscape.
- **What's the minimum useful addition?** Maybe it's not a new platform. Maybe it's closing the loop on the HelpLine's referrals, or making the Census real-time instead of annual, or giving CDCs a lightweight way to report outcomes that feeds into Data Driven Detroit's existing portal.
- **Is the barrier technical or political?** Houston succeeded because of top-down political will. If Detroit's 30+ orgs don't want to share data (funding competition, turf, different funder reporting requirements), no amount of good software solves that.
- **Where does this tool complement vs. duplicate?** The screener shouldn't compete with the HelpLine or findhelp. It should produce data those systems can't.

## The ROI Argument

### Direct Repair ROI

- Rebuilding Together study: every $1 in home repair generates **$2.84 in social value**, with $1.32 from saved Medicare/Medicaid costs
- North Carolina Housing Finance Agency: every $1 on urgent repairs saves **$19 in Medicare/Medicaid costs**

### The Real ROI: Data That Wins Funding

The strongest argument isn't about operational efficiency. It's about leverage.

CDCs that can show demand data win more funding:
- Delaware Habitat's statewide needs assessment (25,000 homes, $96M cost) led to a statewide healthy homes program design
- St. Louis UMSL research ($300M citywide need) catalyzed the Home Repair Network
- Detroit's own census data ($63M vs. $2-4B need, 14,000 waitlist for 1,000 slots) is the kind of data that moves funders — but only when collected systematically

If a CDC can show: "500 residents screened eligible for lead abatement, 80 served, 420 still waiting — that's $6.3M in unmet demand at $15K/home" — that's a fundable gap with a dollar figure. No CDC in Detroit can produce that sentence today.

### The Prevention Math

Every resident connected to HOPE before they enter tax delinquency saves:
- $1,500-3,000/year in their pocket (retained spending in the neighborhood)
- $21,556 in avoided demolition costs if the alternative is vacancy
- 20% preserved property value for surrounding homes
- Retained tax base, school enrollment, commercial viability

## Where Neighborhood Navigator Fits

The tool addresses two layers of this problem:

**Layer 1: Resident navigation (Point 5a from the job listing)**
Help residents find programs they qualify for. The screener handles this with client-side matching against 30 Detroit programs. No server needed, no PII collected, works at a community event on a phone.

**Layer 2: Demand quantification (Point 5b from the job listing)**
Track what residents need vs. what they can access. Anonymous screening events log to Supabase. The dashboard shows: how many people screened, what programs matched, what confidence level, which ZIPs — and critically, the gap between "screened eligible" and "actually served" when CDCs update intake outcomes.

This is the data no one in Detroit has at the individual-CDC level, let alone aggregated across the ecosystem.

## Current Assessment (March 2026)

### How We Got Here

The project started from two bullet points in a part-time CDC program manager job listing. Point 5 mentioned helping residents apply for programs and tracking which ones need more funding. That sounded like a tool problem, so we built an eligibility screener.

But that was a solution built from the outside in — starting with what seemed useful from a brief job description before understanding the actual problem landscape. The introductory conversation with Mac revealed this isn't a single-CDC tooling gap. It's a citywide data infrastructure problem across 30+ organizations spending $63M/year with no shared tracking. Mac connecting us to D4 (a metro-level policy org, not another neighborhood CDC) confirmed the scope is bigger than a screener.

### What the Screener Is and Isn't

**What it is:**
- A working proof of concept that demonstrates technical credibility in the space
- A useful tool for community events (phone-friendly, no PII, instant results)
- A concrete demo for conversations with D4 and other stakeholders
- A foundation with multi-tenant architecture already built

**What it isn't:**
- The product that solves the systemic problem. The screener helps individual residents. The systemic problem is that nobody has real-time demand data across the ecosystem.
- Differentiated from existing tools. The Detroit Housing HelpLine (6,700 calls/month, built with Harvard Kennedy School) already does resident navigation. So does findhelp.org, 211, and the Detroit Housing Network.

### The Real Product

The gap analysis dashboard — not the screener — is where the unmet value lives. But the dashboard has problems:

1. **Volume dependency.** A single CDC at quarterly events generates maybe a few hundred screenings/year. Not enough to move a funder. Value comes from aggregation across multiple CDCs, which requires adoption that hasn't happened.
2. **The outcome loop isn't closed.** Phase 2 (intake bridge) is only foundation-laid. Without tracking screened → contacted → applied → served, you can say "500 matched for lead abatement" but not "500 matched, 80 served, 420 still waiting." The second sentence wins funding. The first is a marketing stat.
3. **Program data maintenance.** 30 programs in static JSON with no update strategy. Who maintains accuracy when programs open, close, change eligibility, or exhaust funding? Stale data destroys trust.

### What We Know vs. Don't Know

**Know for sure:**
- Nobody has real-time demand data across Detroit's home repair ecosystem
- The 2024 Census proved the data is valuable but took a manual annual effort by Data Driven Detroit
- CDCs can't produce the "X screened, Y served, Z waiting" sentence funders need
- Mac sees us as the IT infrastructure person and is making introductions on that basis

**Don't know yet:**
- What D4 actually wants from a collaboration — policy research support? A data tool? Something else?
- Whether the 30+ orgs would adopt a shared system, or whether fragmentation is partly political (turf, funding competition, different funder reporting requirements)
- Where Data Driven Detroit fits — potential partner, or do they already own this lane?
- What Mac's Zoho system actually looks like and how far along it is
- What format D4's policy briefs and funder conversations take (the output we should work backward from)

### Next Move

**Don't build anything else until after the D4 conversation.** Go in with questions, not a product demo. Find out:
- What does their advocacy work look like day-to-day?
- What data do they wish they had that doesn't exist?
- What do they think the real coordination barriers are?
- How does the Home Repair Census get produced and what are its limitations?
- Is there appetite among the 30+ orgs for shared infrastructure, or is that a political minefield?

The answer to "what should this tool become" comes from that conversation more than from more building.

## Personal Positioning & Why This Project

### The Structural Translation

Strip the domain jargon and this is the same work done at 31st Street Capital, applied to civic services:

| Commercial Concept | Detroit Home Repair Equivalent |
|-------------------|-------------------------------|
| Lead gen funnel | Resident discovers program → screens → intake → connected to provider |
| Attribution / outcome tracking | Did the referral convert? Was the resident served? Which programs have capacity? |
| Multi-location ops (44 stores, 6 systems) | 30+ independent orgs that need coordinated reporting without centralized authority |
| Campaign budget optimization | "Which programs need more funding?" — same gap analysis, different stakeholder |
| CRM pipeline reporting | Screened → contacted → applied → served → gap = unmet demand |

The actual experience — connected platforms, data pipelines, CRM, multi-stakeholder coordination, making data flow across fragmented organizations — transfers directly. Different nouns, same verbs.

### Dual Purpose

This project serves two goals, and being honest about both is important:

**Community investment.** Lafayette Park resident who wants to invest in the city and move it forward. The problem is real, the people are real, and the gap in infrastructure is something I can actually help with. This gives purpose beyond paid work.

**Portfolio proof point.** "Designed the data infrastructure connecting 30+ organizations in Detroit's $63M home repair ecosystem" demonstrates systems thinking, multi-stakeholder coordination, and measurable civic impact. It works as a case study for enterprise roles too — the structural problem (fragmented organizations, no shared data, outcome tracking gaps) is identical to what Fortune 1000 companies face across divisions and vendors.

Both motivations are valid. The risk is letting either one crowd out the other — over-investing time that should go to paid advisory work, or treating the community as a portfolio exercise. The balance comes from scoping involvement appropriately after the D4 conversation.

### How to Show Up for D4

**Lead with:** Local resident, problem-led. "I saw a structural problem through a local CDC, built something to test the idea, learned the problem is bigger than one org, and want to understand the real landscape."

**Lean into:** Systems thinker who builds things. The screener is a working demo — didn't write a white paper, built a tool. In the CDC world, that carries more weight than methodology decks.

**Don't lead with:** AI, enterprise consulting, transformation methodology, the playbook. Nothing about the immediate problem requires AI. It requires data infrastructure, coordination design, and change management. The AI angle comes later (if at all). Leading with it signals "solution looking for a problem."

**The throughline that works across both worlds:** "I've been solving coordination problems across fragmented organizations for a decade. The domain changes — retail, hospitality, now civic infrastructure — the structural problem doesn't. Technology is 10%, getting organizations to change is 90%."
